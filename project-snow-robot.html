An electric snow robot for all four seasons. Three days, AI doing the CAD, me directing every decision.

Five people, one brief. I owned the machine.

The client's snow robot runs on gas and rubber tracks. The brief was to make it autonomous and useful all year. We split the work by discipline, and I took the mechanical half: the body, how it moves, how it docks and how it is built.

Electric, not gas

Only an electric robot can charge itself, and real autonomy needs that. It is not cheaper upfront. It wins on running cost.

A fence of beacon stakes electrical team

UWB and RTK stakes at the corners draw an invisible wall that keeps working when snow or grass blinds the cameras.

Wheels on lifting pods

Tracks tear a lawn every time they turn. Wheels do not, and a pod that lifts 180 mm lets the robot climb out of a plow berm by itself.

One head, four seasons

The housing rotates 90 degrees and the rotor swaps: auger for snow, flail for grass, brush for leaves. One motor, one shaft.

I decided

  • Wheels over tracks.The mechanical direction, backed with traction and torque numbers.
  • The bar for the look.EcoFlow Blade and Altoz as the reference, and a no for every render that fell short.
  • A rotating head.Manual 90 degree turn with an index plunger and a damper, so a 20 to 30 kg head cannot drop on a hand.
  • Plastic mower blades.A paddle that breaks does not throw metal, and blades become a consumable.
  • Plug-in charging.A floating plug in the dock instead of wireless, so the robot reverses in and connects itself.
  • Never edit a version.Every iteration is a copy, so every earlier one stays comparable.

The AI executed

  • Parametric CAD.One script per version, dimensions at the top, exported as STL for rendering and STEP for SolidWorks.
  • Render scripts.Studio shots, photoreal snow scenes and animation, run on my own GPU.
  • Parallel variants.Four architectures built and rendered side by side in about half an hour.
  • The calculations.Traction, torque and energy, from the assumptions I fixed.
  • The exploded view.The team's parts list placed inside the machine and annotated.

Electrical and software teammates supplied sensing, compute and the BOM. I fitted their parts inside the machine.

AI did not replace the designer. It changed the job. Give direction, say what is good and what is not, repeat until it is right.

This is why iteration is cheap. Changing the lift travel is one edit, and every render updates.

Finding that changed the design

The snow head strikes any ramp steeper than about 13 to 14 degrees. Either the head lifts into its transport pose before docking, or the ramp stays at 13 degrees or less.

My own cost table is blunt. At production cost multiples, all three docks cost more than a complete Yarbo robot. A consumer would not pay that, so the next step is a lean version without the extras.

The first charging animation explained nothing.

Only red and orange springs glowing are visible. Nothing visibly connects to anything. My feedback after watching it

What was wrong

  • The bezel and float plate sat between the camera and the pins.
  • The orange springs were the most saturated thing in frame, so they read as the subject.
  • The socket was seen at a grazing angle.
  • The LED change was too subtle.

What changed

  • A true side profile, perpendicular to the mating axis.
  • A half-section cut through socket, bezel and plug, so pins slide into sleeves in view.
  • Steel gray springs, a slower last 30 mm, a one second hold on the mated state.
  • A larger LED ring that turns green only when the pilot pin confirms.
  1. Shutter opensA cover keeps snow and salt out of the robot's socket.
  2. Plug self-centersA floating plate on four springs absorbs 10 to 15 mm of misalignment.
  3. Pilot confirmsA shorter third pin makes contact last.
  4. Power goes liveThe charger energizes only after the pilot confirms.

The decision the numbers drive. The robot is traction-limited, not motor-limited. So it clears early, when snow is thin, it runs chains in winter, and it keeps its weight over the wheels. The blower also takes about 80% of the energy, so it sets the battery size, not the wheels.

What is not proven yet.

This is a concept, and I would rather say where it is weak than have someone find it.

  • Estimated inputs.Mass, auger intake force and battery capacity are estimates, not measurements.
  • Visualization-grade CAD.Parts and surfaces built for form and packaging. No FEA and no physical test.
  • Untested in the cold.Traction on packed snow, auger load in wet snow and seals at -30 °C still need testing.
  • Sealing.The lift pods and the connector against salt and ice are the biggest open engineering problem.
  • Dock cost.Priced above what a home owner would pay. A lean version is the next step.
  • Grass bag.Still a plain box in every variant.
  • Outdoors only.The robot never enters the house and stairs are dropped from the design.
  • Benchmarks.Compared against Yarbo and Snowbot S1. Look references: EcoFlow Blade, Altoz and the Harto Plow concept.
